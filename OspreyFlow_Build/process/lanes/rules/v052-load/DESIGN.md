At the recommended `--graph-scale 0.25`: 162,600 nodes written (about 142,000 distinct), 289,200
links, 13,600 SAME_AS, 18,000 texts, and the same 100 million analytics rows (measured: the
generator wrote this shape at `--scale 0.02` as 531 MB in 16 s on an 8-job Mac; the analytics files
are 186 to 215 bytes a row, so 19.6 GB at `--scale 1`).

**Shared nodes.** The subscribers' numbers and handsets are written again by the calls and the
sightings under the same ids with the same marking, so such a node is in two datasets, as a
re-ingested record is (the upsert adds the dataset; ADR-099). Nothing with a different marking
shares a node: a SECRET source's record of a number or an account is a node of its own, which is
what makes the cross-access merge candidates.

**Why the big source is rows, not nodes.** The call records are 60 million analytics rows
(`AnalyticsPort.load_events`), and the graph holds one `COMMUNICATED_WITH` link per calling pair
with its count and first and last call, which is the shape PaySim already loads (ADR-055: every
row as an event, the graph a summary) and the shape the product reads (a link's events are its
occurrences). Writing tens of millions of nodes or links through the adapter is not possible in
the time ("Wall time"). Each link's `occurrences` and interval are exactly its rows' (a test).

## Names and the plants for search

A synthetic population of 150,000 people (37,500 at 0.25) is drawn from 50 forename groups and
40 surname groups of spellings (Mohammed, Muhammad, Mohamed, Mohamad, Mohammad, Muhammed;
Smith, Smyth, Smithe; Chaudhry, Choudhury, Chowdhury; Al-Hassan, Alhassan, El Hassan; Zoë, Zoe;
Łukasz, Lukasz; Dvořák, Dvorak, and so on) and 600 invented surnames made from syllables (checked
against the Prison Intelligence Dataset's names, which no other file may use). Each source records
a share of the population under its own ids, and spells each name its own way: 42 % as the
canonical spelling, 30 % another forename of the group, 20 % another surname of the group or a
sound-alike rewrite (ph/f, ck/k, y/ie, c/k), 8 % a one-letter slip (two letters swapped, one
dropped, one doubled). A document names its subjects either as their record does or with a
spelling of its own, so the full text holds variants too. Nominals carry nicknames and alias
spellings; the watchlist carries one to three alias spellings and a `SURNAME, Forename` form.
Organisations are named differently by the register, the SARs and the intelligence reports
("Northgate Logistics Ltd", "Northgate Logistics Limited", "North gate Logistics Ltd.").
Numbers are written three ways (`07700 123456`, `+44 7700 123456`, `447700123456`) with one
normalised value. Dates: dates of birth, link intervals, document `sent_at` and event
`occurred_at`, all in January 2025 to November 2026.

**Nothing real** (rule 9; a test scans every generated file): numbers are `+447700…` outside the
drama block `07700 900xxx` the PID uses; NINOs carry only prefixes HMRC never allocates (TN, NT,
BG, GB, KN, NK, ZZ); postcodes start `ZZ`, no UK area; IBANs use the invented bank code `XSYN`;
IMEIs start `35999`; e-mail addresses are at `example.org` or `example.net`; the prose is
templated and invented.

## Narrative documents

72,000 at graph scale 1 (18,000 at 0.25): 40,000 intelligence reports (mean 3.7 KB),
15,000 witness statements and case notes (mean 6.7 KB), 12,000 SARs (mean 1.8 KB) and 5,000 covert
source reports (mean 2.2 KB), measured at 0.25; about 280 MB of text at graph scale 1. Each is a `Document` node (`title`,
`sent_at`, `media_kind: text`) linked `MENTIONED_IN` from its two to five subjects, and its full
text goes to `document_texts` held by the document's owning unit, which ADR-150 indexes as a text
document of its own. The text is templated prose: four sets of paragraph templates (8 to 18
each), filled from the subjects' spellings, nicknames and dates of birth, and the source's places,
organisations, numbers and accounts, with invented vehicles, sums, officers and dates.

## Merge candidates (SAME_AS)

Planted, not left to resolution: written as the resolver writes one (`same_as_writer.py`'s
shape): `inferred` (rule 5), `score`, `model_version: client-size-plant-1`, a
`comparison_vector`, marked as the more restrictive of the two records (its unit kept, by the
scheme's merge rule), and in no dataset when the records are in different ones (ADR-099). Each
plant file keeps a `truth` column (`same` or `different`) that never reaches the store: it is the
answer key step 4 can measure the merged view against.

- People, eight source pairs, most across access: nominals with custody, intelligence and the
  watchlist; intelligence with covert reporting; subscribers with transactions; case files with
  nominals; transactions with SARs; company officers with SARs. A share (40 to 70 %) of the
  people the two sources share are planted, scored by how far each spelling is (0.999 for the same
  spelling, down to about 0.83 for a slip). 8 % are near misses: another person with the same
  surname, scored 0.35 to 0.65.
- Organisations: each SAR and intelligence organisation with its register entry (same
  `org_ref`, another name style), scored 0.95 to 0.99.
- Accounts and numbers: each SAR account with the bank's record of the same IBAN, and each
  intelligence number with the network's, scored 0.999.

## Determinism and the generator

`python -m scripts.generate_client_size --out DIR --scale 1 --graph-scale 0.25 --seed 20261116
--jobs 15`. Every value is drawn from a generator seeded by a SHA-256 of the seed and the thing's
name (the master person, the record, the document), so a row does not depend on any other row,
the order of writing, or the job count; the same arguments write the same bytes (a test, at one
and three jobs). `--out` is refused inside the git working tree and when it already holds a set.
It streams: a part file is written row by row and hashed as it goes; nothing holds more than one
row. `manifest.json` lists every file with its rows, bytes and SHA-256, the seed, both scales and
each source's dataset and marking. At `--scale 1` it is about 2,000 CPU-seconds, so about three
minutes on the server's 16 cores.

## The load

From `OspreyFlow_Core` on the server, with the leased stack's ports (`stack_lease.sh ports rules`),
its passwords and `OSPREY_ANALYTICS_ENGINE=clickhouse` in the environment, as the live tests run,
in a `tmux` session since it runs for hours:
`PYTHONPATH=.:packages .venv/bin/python -m scripts.load_client_size --from DIR [--grant ACCOUNT=LEVEL …]
[--stages …]`. The stages run in this order, each file by file:

1. **cases**: each dataset registered, held by its source's unit, and read by a case of its own
   ("Client-size: …", `case_seed.case_from_arguments`, as every seed), shared as `--grant` says.
2. **nodes**: through `stamping(adapter, dataset=…)`, `upsert_nodes` in chunks of 40
   (`seed_support.write_graph`). 20,000 rows a file.
3. **edges**: the same stamping graph, the adapter's bulk loader (ADR-058) in chunks of 100. All
   nodes are written before any link, so every end exists.
4. **same_as**: through the unstamped adapter (a cross-dataset candidate is in no dataset), bulk.
5. **texts**: `ExtractionCandidateStore.store_document_text`, one upsert each, as the owning unit.
6. **events**: `AnalyticsPort.load_events` in batches of 50,000, each file under its own
   `load_id` (`client-size:<path>`), scoped to its source's case. The engine is the stack's
   (`OSPREY_ANALYTICS_ENGINE`; the lane stacks run ClickHouse, which takes JSONEachRow inserts).
7. **check**: the scan for link ids stored twice every seed ends with.

**Resumable.** `load_state.json` beside the files records each file as started and as done; a
rerun skips what is done. A file that started and did not finish is redone safely: nodes are an
upsert keyed on id; a links file is bulk-loaded again with what is already stored handed back as
collisions and skipped, never merged (the generator writes every link once, so a collision on a
rerun is the file's own earlier write; merging it would double its count, which a test shows red);
an events file's partial rows are first removed by its `load_id` (`retract_load`). A ledger of
another set (seed, scale or version), a changed file (SHA-256) and a deployment ceiling below
SECRET are refused before anything is written.

**Afterwards.** Step 3c's outbox (ADR-150) records every node the load writes, so the index
follows the load; run `python -m scripts.rebuild_search_index` once the load is done (faster than
draining a quarter of a million outbox rows through the indexer) and then the drift check, which
must report no difference before step 4 measures.

## Wall time

The graph is the bottleneck, and the reason the recommended load is `--graph-scale 0.25`. Every
id lookup the adapter's write path sends is an unlabelled Gremlin `has("id", …)`, which on ArcadeDB
26.10.1 scans every vertex (ADR-058 Amendment 9, ADR-124 Amendment 2: one lookup 11 ms at 20,000
vertices, 77 ms at 200,000, about 0.37 µs a vertex). `upsert_nodes` sends one per node and the bulk
loader's chunk script two per link (`GremlinBulkTransport.write_chunk`), so the cost grows with the
square of the graph:

| Graph scale | Distinct nodes | Links (with SAME_AS) | Nodes | Links | Analytics, texts, check | Total |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 0.25 (recommended) | 142,000 | 303,000 | about 1.5 h | about 9 h | about 1.5 h | **about 12 h** |
| 0.5 | 285,000 | 605,000 | about 5 h | about 36 h | about 1.5 h | about 42 h |
| 1.0 | 570,000 | 1,210,000 | about 17 h | about 6 days | about 1.5 h | not feasible |

These are extrapolations from those single-lookup timings, not measurements of this load, and
could be out by a factor of two either way. **So the load starts with a calibration:** generate at
`--graph-scale 0.02` into a second folder and load it into the leased stack first (about 15
minutes), read the loader's per-file rates, and confirm or lower 0.25 before the real load; then
reset the stack (the calibration set's elements are in the same `cs-` datasets). The analytics
rows (about 100 million) go at ClickHouse's JSONEachRow speed, bounded by building and validating
each `EventRecord` in one Python process: estimated 20,000 to 40,000 a second, so 45 minutes to
1.5 hours.

## What the load needs from the stack

- **Disk:** about 20 GB for the generated files (outside the checkout), and in the stores about
  3 to 5 GB of ClickHouse (100 million rows, compressed), 1 to 2 GB of ArcadeDB, 0.1 GB of Postgres
  text, 0.5 GB of OpenSearch: **45 GB free** on the server, with headroom for ClickHouse merges.
- **Memory:** ArcadeDB at the dev stack's `-Xmx8G` (`deploy/compose/dev.yaml`; `harness/profiles.py`
  gives 8 GiB for local-lite, 15 GiB for small). The loader holds one file at a time (20,000 rows
  at most for the graph, one 50,000-row batch for events).
- **The ceiling:** `OSPREY_HIGHEST_MARKING` unset or SECRET (the loader refuses otherwise).
- **Accounts for step 4:** the leak test needs people in these units: unit-records, unit-custody,
  unit-economic, unit-comms, unit-fiu, unit-intel, unit-intel-covert, unit-investigations,
  unit-covert, unit-border, at OFFICIAL, OFFICIAL-SENSITIVE and SECRET, with and without code R.
- **The stack kept up:** one long lease for the calibration, the load (about 12 hours), the index
  rebuild and the drift check, and then left loaded for step 4.

## Not decided here (each with the default built)

1. **The graph's size.** Default `--graph-scale 0.25` on the existing write path, about 12 hours.
   The alternative is to make the adapter's write path resolve ids by SQL on each type's unique
   index (what ADR-124 Amendment 2 did for `get_nodes`), which should make 1.0 a few hours'
   load; that is an adapter change with its conformance cases first and an ADR amendment, not
   built here. Recommended: load 0.25 by Wed 18 Nov; take the lookup fix as its own record.
2. **Suggestions with one type in the tens of millions** (ROUTE_052 search scope item 5). This set
   cannot give it under either option: the tens of millions are analytics rows, not nodes, and
   the largest node type is Person (70,250 at 0.25; about 281,000 at 1.0). Recommended: step 4
   measures suggestions on what is loaded, and the owner decides whether a separate index-only
   corpus of ten million generated names is worth building for the latency figure alone.
3. **The numbers' range.** Built: `+447700` numbers outside the drama block `07700 900xxx`; only
   that block is Ofcom's reserved range, and 999,000 numbers do not fit in it. Recommended: keep;
   the alternative is a fictional country code, which the search's number canonicalisation may
   treat differently from a UK one.
4. **One case per dataset.** Built: twelve cases, one per dataset. Step 4 makes any composite it
   needs (ADR-099 Amendment 12).
